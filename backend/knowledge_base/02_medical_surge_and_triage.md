# Mass Casualty Triage and Medical Surge Management

## 1. Triage categories

Field triage sorts casualties by survivability benefit per unit of resource,
not by severity alone:

- **RED (Immediate)** — Life-threatening but survivable with prompt
  intervention. Airway compromise, severe haemorrhage, tension pneumothorax.
- **YELLOW (Delayed)** — Significant injury, but intervention can safely wait
  hours. Stable fractures, moderate burns without airway involvement.
- **GREEN (Minor)** — Ambulatory, minor injury. Often the largest group and
  frequently the first to self-present at hospitals, where they consume
  capacity needed for RED cases.
- **BLACK (Expectant)** — Injuries incompatible with survival given available
  resources. This category exists to protect resources for salvageable
  casualties and must be applied by qualified clinical staff only.

## 2. Casualty distribution rule

The most important surge principle is that casualties must be **distributed**,
not concentrated. Left uncoordinated, the majority of casualties self-present
to the single nearest facility, overwhelming it while facilities twenty minutes
further away sit underused.

Distribution logic:

- Route RED casualties to the nearest **trauma-capable** facility with
  confirmed available capacity, not simply the nearest facility.
- Route GREEN casualties away from trauma centres entirely, to primary health
  centres or field medical posts.
- Confirm receiving capacity before dispatch. Sending casualties to a facility
  already at capacity converts one overwhelmed hospital into two.
- Maintain a reserve: do not allocate the final ICU bed or ventilator in a
  district to a single incident while the hazard is still escalating.

## 3. Ambulance planning ratios

For planning purposes, one ambulance sustains approximately two casualty
transfers per hour over short urban distances, degrading sharply with hazard
conditions. Required ambulances should be computed from projected RED and
YELLOW casualties and the round-trip time to receiving facilities, and should
never be estimated as a fixed fraction of total casualties.

## 4. Blood and consumables

Whole-blood demand in trauma-heavy events runs well above baseline. O-negative
supply is the binding constraint in the first hours because it is issued before
cross-matching. Blood banks should be alerted at the same moment field triage
begins, not after transfusion demand materialises.

## 5. Facility degradation

A hospital is a piece of infrastructure and can itself become a casualty.
Assess for each receiving facility:

- Is the facility inside the projected hazard zone?
- Does it hold functioning backup power, and fuel for at least 72 hours?
- Is its access road passable for ambulances under current conditions?
- Is its water supply intact? Loss of water forces surgical suspension faster
  than loss of power.

A facility that is nearer but compromised is worse than useless as a referral
destination, because casualties routed there must be moved a second time.

## 6. Post-event health surveillance

Communicable disease risk rises after flooding and population displacement.
Establish surveillance for water-borne illness (cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A),
vector-borne illness (dengue, malaria, chikungunya) and leptospirosis, which is
strongly associated with flood-water exposure. Surveillance should begin during
the response phase, not after it.
