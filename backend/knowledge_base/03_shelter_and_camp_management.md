# Relief Camp and Shelter Management Minimum Standards

## 1. Quantified minimum standards

These figures are the widely used humanitarian planning minimums and should
drive resource requirement calculations directly:

| Provision | Minimum standard |
| --- | --- |
| Drinking water | 15 litres per person per day |
| Water for survival (first 48h, absolute floor) | 7.5 litres per person per day |
| Covered living space | 3.5 m² per person |
| Total site area | 45 m² per person where land permits |
| Toilets | 1 per 20 people, sex-segregated |
| Water points | 1 per 250 people |
| Distance to water point | Not more than 500 m from any dwelling |
| Distance to toilet | Not more than 50 m from any dwelling |

Camps consistently under-provision water and sanitation relative to food,
because food shortfalls generate immediate visible complaint while sanitation
shortfalls generate delayed disease outbreaks. Allocation logic must resist
this bias explicitly.

## 2. Site selection

A shelter site must be verified against the hazard, not merely designated in
advance:

- Outside the projected inundation zone, with margin for forecast escalation.
- Above the highest recorded flood level for the locality where known.
- On ground with drainage; standing water inside a camp drives disease.
- Reachable by heavy vehicle for supply delivery under current conditions.
- Not dependent on a single access route that the hazard can sever.

Pre-designating schools and community halls is sound practice, but a
pre-designated site inside the flood plain must be reclassified before an
event, not discovered to be unusable during one.

## 3. Vulnerable groups

Camp planning must specifically account for:

- Unaccompanied minors — require immediate registration and protection.
- Elderly and persons with disability — require accessible sanitation and
  ground-floor placement.
- Pregnant and lactating women — require privacy, and proximity to a medical
  post.
- Persons with chronic conditions — dialysis, insulin dependence, tuberculosis
  and HIV treatment continuity are frequently interrupted by displacement and
  cause preventable deaths well after the hazard has passed.

Equitable provision is not a secondary consideration. Camps that fail
vulnerable groups produce the majority of post-event mortality.

## 4. Capacity accounting

Stated capacity and usable capacity differ. Usable capacity must discount:

- Space occupied by kitchens, stores, and medical posts.
- Sections without roof integrity or lighting.
- Areas that flood at lower thresholds than the main structure.

When aggregate spare capacity across reachable shelters is less than the
population requiring shelter, this is a **capacity deficit** and must be
escalated immediately rather than absorbed by over-crowding. Overcrowding
beyond 3.5 m² per person converts a shelter into a disease vector.

## 5. Camp closure

Camps should not close before return conditions are verified: structural safety
of dwellings, restored water supply, and cleared access. Premature closure to
free resources produces secondary displacement and repeat caseload.
