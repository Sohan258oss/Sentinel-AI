# Relief Resource Allocation and Multi-Agency Coordination Doctrine

## 1. Allocation priority order

When demand exceeds supply — which is the normal condition in a major event —
allocate in this order:

1. **Life safety** — Search and rescue, medical evacuation, immediate
   life-sustaining supply (water, critical medicine).
2. **Incident stabilisation** — Preventing escalation: dewatering, firebreaks,
   structural shoring, epidemic prevention.
3. **Property and environment** — Asset protection once life safety is assured.

No quantity of second-tier need outranks an unmet first-tier need. A hundred
blankets do not outrank one unfilled rescue-boat requirement.

## 2. Prioritisation within a tier

Within a priority tier, rank competing requirements by:

- **Beneficiaries per unit** — how many people one unit serves.
- **Time criticality** — deadline before the need becomes irreversible.
- **Substitutability** — whether another resource can serve the same need.
- **Access decay** — whether the delivery route is degrading. A requirement in
  an area about to become unreachable must be served before one that will
  remain reachable, even at equal urgency.

The last point is routinely missed. Allocation that ignores closing access
windows produces plans that are optimal on paper and undeliverable in practice.

## 3. Sourcing rules

- Draw from the **nearest depot with stock**, adjusted for mobilisation delay.
  A depot 40 km away with a 15-minute mobilisation beats one 20 km away with a
  90-minute mobilisation.
- Do not exhaust a single depot. Depleting the nearest store leaves no capacity
  for the next incident, which in a multi-hazard event is often hours away.
- Preserve a strategic reserve of no less than 15% of district stock for
  emerging incidents unless the current event is already catastrophic.
- Prefer multi-organisation sourcing. Drawing from government, NGO and
  volunteer stores in parallel is faster than sequentially exhausting one.

## 4. Multi-agency coordination

Effective response is multi-organisational by necessity. Each partner brings
distinct capability:

- **State disaster authority** — bulk relief material, statutory coordination.
- **National response force** — technical rescue, swift-water capability,
  specialist search teams.
- **Municipal bodies** — local knowledge, dewatering, sanitation, camp running.
- **Hospitals** — clinical capacity, blood, ambulances, medical teams.
- **NGOs** — surge distribution capacity, community access, speed.
- **Volunteer groups** — labour at scale, local navigation, last-mile reach.
- **Fire and rescue services** — extraction, firefighting, pumping capacity.

Coordination failure, not resource scarcity, is the most common cause of poor
outcomes. Duplicate delivery to accessible areas alongside zero delivery to
cut-off areas is the characteristic signature of uncoordinated response.

## 5. Honest shortfall reporting

An allocation plan must state what it cannot cover. Unmet needs must be
recorded with:

- The quantity short.
- The population affected by the shortfall.
- The consequence if it remains unmet.
- The escalation path — neighbouring district, state authority, national force,
  central government.

A plan that silently drops uncoverable requirements creates a false picture of
adequacy and delays escalation. Escalation delayed is the most expensive error
in disaster logistics.

## 6. Reassessment cadence

Allocation is not a one-time computation. Re-plan when:

- Hazard severity changes by one level.
- A delivery route becomes impassable.
- Actual casualty or displacement counts diverge from projection by over 25%.
- New depot capacity becomes available.

At minimum, re-plan every six hours during active response.
