# Critical Infrastructure Damage Assessment

## 1. Lifeline priority order

Restore and protect in this sequence, because each depends on those above it:

1. **Access routes** — Nothing else can be restored if crews cannot reach it.
2. **Water supply** — Loss forces hospital and camp failure within 24 hours.
3. **Power** — Determines hospital, pumping and communication viability.
4. **Telecommunications** — Determines coordination capability.
5. **Sanitation and drainage** — Determines epidemic risk over days.

## 2. Structural triage convention

Rapid assessment classifies structures into three states:

- **Safe (green)** — No significant structural damage; occupancy permitted.
- **Restricted (yellow)** — Damage present; limited or short-duration entry
  only, pending detailed evaluation.
- **Unsafe (red)** — Collapse or partial collapse risk; entry prohibited
  including for retrieval of belongings.

Placarding must be physical and visible at the structure. Verbal advice is not
retained by displaced populations under stress.

## 3. Road and bridge assessment

Roads fail in ways that are not visible from a vehicle:

- **Scour** — Water erodes support beneath an intact-looking surface. A road
  that appears passable may collapse under load. Treat any road that has been
  submerged as suspect until inspected.
- **Approach embankment failure** — The most common bridge failure mode is not
  the span but the approach. Inspect approaches specifically.
- **Debris damming** — Accumulated debris at a bridge pier converts the bridge
  into a dam, raising upstream levels and increasing lateral load until
  failure. Debris accumulation is an urgent finding, not a cosmetic one.

Load restrictions must be assumed, not waived, for relief convoys. A heavy
supply vehicle collapsing a marginal bridge severs the route for everyone.

## 4. Power system hazards

- Do not deploy rescue teams into inundated areas before supply isolation is
  confirmed with the distribution utility.
- Downed conductors in standing water energise the water surface over a wide
  radius, well beyond visible contact.
- Restoration sequencing must prioritise hospitals, water pumping stations and
  telecommunications sites over general distribution, even where general
  restoration would serve more customers per unit effort.

## 5. Access corridor identification

The single most valuable infrastructure product for the commander is a list of
**corridors that remain viable**, not a list of what is broken. For each
affected settlement, determine:

- At least one currently passable route, with its load limit.
- The expected time until that route degrades, if it is degrading.
- The alternative if it fails.

A settlement with no identified viable corridor must be flagged as
**isolated**, which changes its resource requirement entirely: it now needs
air or water delivery and self-sufficiency stock, not a truck convoy.

## 6. Imagery-based assessment

Aerial and ground imagery accelerates assessment but has known failure modes:

- Water depth cannot be reliably estimated from overhead imagery alone.
- Tree and cloud cover conceal damage, producing false negatives.
- Undamaged roofs frequently conceal severe internal and lower-storey damage.

Imagery findings should be treated as indicative and prioritised for physical
verification, never as a substitute for it. Any automated damage classification
must report its confidence and must not be used as the sole basis for declaring
a structure safe.
