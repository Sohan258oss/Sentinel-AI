# Emergency Risk Communication and Rumour Management

## 1. Message construction

An effective public warning contains five elements. Messages missing any of
them measurably reduce protective action:

1. **Hazard** — What is happening, specifically.
2. **Location** — Precisely who is affected, by named area.
3. **Timing** — When impact is expected, and how long there is to act.
4. **Action** — Exactly what to do, as an imperative.
5. **Source** — Which authority issued this.

Vague warnings produce inaction. "Residents are advised to take necessary
precautions" is not a warning; it transfers the decision burden back to a
population without the information to carry it.

## 2. Message discipline

- State uncertainty explicitly rather than concealing it. Populations tolerate
  acknowledged uncertainty; they do not forgive discovered concealment.
- Never issue an all-clear that has not been verified. A premature all-clear
  causes return movement into an active hazard and is among the most lethal
  communication errors.
- Repeat consistently across channels. Contradiction between channels destroys
  credibility faster than silence.
- Issue in the languages actually spoken in the affected area, including
  through non-text channels for low-literacy and visually impaired populations.

## 3. Audience differentiation

The same situation requires materially different messages:

- **Public** — Short, imperative, actionable. Focus on what to do now.
- **Responders** — Tactical detail, hazards to responders, assembly points,
  command structure and reporting lines.
- **Government** — Situation assessment, resource status, unmet needs,
  escalation requests, decisions required.
- **Hospitals** — Expected casualty volume and category, timing, referral
  routing changes.
- **Volunteers** — Specific taskings, required skills, safety limits, and an
  explicit statement of what they must NOT attempt.

The volunteer safety boundary is essential. Untrained volunteers attempting
water rescue is a recurring source of preventable responder deaths.

## 4. Rumour and misinformation management

Predictable rumour categories emerge in most events and should be pre-empted
rather than merely rebutted:

- Dam-failure rumours during flooding, which trigger mass panic movement.
- Casualty figures wildly divergent from confirmed counts.
- False shortage claims driving hoarding of fuel, water and medicine.
- Fabricated official instructions, including false evacuation or all-clear
  orders.
- Water-contamination claims, true or false, which suppress use of safe supply.

Counter-rumour practice: lead with the verified fact, state it plainly, and
avoid repeating the false claim prominently. Repetition of a falsehood, even to
deny it, increases its retention. Name the authoritative channel and direct
people to it.

## 5. Communication during infrastructure failure

Assume telecommunications degradation. Maintain non-network channels: public
address, community radio, loudhailer, physical notice at camps, and
runner-based messaging to isolated settlements. A communication plan dependent
on the mobile network fails at exactly the moment it is needed.
