# Multi-Hazard Response Quick Reference

## Cyclone

- Evacuation must complete before sustained winds reach 60 km/h. After that
  point, moving people is more dangerous than sheltering them in place.
- Storm surge, not wind, causes the majority of cyclone deaths. Evacuation
  zoning must be driven by surge modelling and elevation, not wind speed.
- The eye passage produces a deceptive calm. Populations emerging during the
  eye are struck by the reversed-direction eyewall. Warnings must state this
  explicitly.
- Secure or remove loose material before onset; airborne debris is the primary
  injury mechanism.

## Earthquake

- The first 24 hours dominate survival outcomes for trapped casualties;
  extraction rates fall sharply thereafter.
- Aftershocks routinely collapse structures weakened but not felled by the
  main shock. Damaged structures must be treated as unsafe for entry even
  where they appear stable.
- Fire following earthquake is a compound hazard driven by ruptured gas supply
  and downed conductors, and can exceed the direct shaking damage.
- Do not permit re-occupancy of yellow-placarded structures until aftershock
  probability declines and engineering assessment is complete.

## Wildfire and structural fire

- Fire spreads fastest uphill and with wind; evacuation routes must not run
  upslope or downwind of the fire front.
- Smoke causes more casualties than flame. Respiratory casualties will exceed
  burn casualties, often substantially, and medical planning must reflect that
  ratio.
- Maintain defensible space around critical facilities and shelters.
- Never route evacuation through a valley or canyon that channels fire.

## Landslide

- Rainfall-triggered slides frequently occur after rain has stopped, as
  saturation propagates. Standing down too early is a common error.
- Indicators warranting immediate evacuation: new ground cracks, tilting trees
  or poles, sudden change in stream turbidity, or a stream running clear then
  abruptly muddy.
- Slides commonly recur in the same location. Prior slide history is the single
  strongest predictor.

## Heatwave

- Risk is driven by wet-bulb conditions, not dry temperature alone. High
  humidity at moderate temperature can exceed the danger of higher dry heat.
- Highest-risk groups: outdoor labourers, elderly living alone, infants, and
  persons on medication affecting thermoregulation.
- Cooling centres must be open during the actual peak hours, typically 11:00 to
  17:00. Facilities open only during office hours provide little protection.

## Industrial chemical release

- Establish an upwind assembly point. Evacuation routes must run crosswind,
  never downwind.
- Do not commit responders before agent identification; entry without correct
  protective equipment converts responders into casualties.
- Decontaminate before hospital transport. An undecontaminated casualty
  contaminates the receiving facility and can force closure of the emergency
  department.

## Epidemic and post-disaster disease

- Displacement and crowding drive transmission more than the original hazard.
- Priority interventions in order: safe water, sanitation, then vaccination
  where applicable, then case management.
- Establish surveillance during response, not after; detection delay is the
  main determinant of outbreak size.
- Post-flood, expect leptospirosis, cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A, and
  vector-borne disease as standing water persists.

## Cascading hazard chains

Single hazards rarely stay single. Anticipate these documented chains:

- Flood → water contamination → epidemic
- Flood → power isolation → hospital and pumping failure
- Earthquake → gas rupture → urban fire → mass casualty
- Cyclone → storm surge → coastal inundation → displacement
- Landslide → river damming → sudden downstream flood on dam breach
- Heatwave → grid overload → power failure → cooling loss → excess mortality

Planning for the primary hazard alone is the most common strategic failure in
disaster response. The secondary hazard is frequently the larger killer.
